# Day 237: Of Restitution

The Black Breviary, Book II, Refusal. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

Cyrus brought out the vessels the king of Babylon had carried off from Jerusalem and set in the house of his own gods. His treasurer counted them out to a prince of Judah, thirty chargers of gold, a thousand of silver, twenty-nine knives, and basins and bowls after them, five thousand four hundred pieces by the list's own total. At the handing over, Ezra records numbers and no speech.

Leviticus requires a man who has defrauded his neighbor to restore the full value and add a fifth to it, paid to the owner on the day his guilt is known, and only then does the ram follow. Even the Catholic catechism, written for a church of confessionals, insists that stolen goods go back.

A statement goes up on a feed, regretting in the passive voice that hurt was caused, and the person who was harmed learns of it at the same moment as a crowd of strangers.

Confession and the feed both send the admission to hearers who are not the injured. The priest hears the guilt in a booth the injured never enters, the public statement apologizes to an audience, and neither is addressed to the one who was robbed. The law in Numbers pairs the confession with a payment to the injured, and lets the priest collect only when the injured man had no kinsman left to receive it. A debt of honor is paid where it was incurred, to the friend whose money you kept or whose work you spoiled, at cost plus a fifth and into the hand.

Charge: Pay what you owe to the one you owe it.

Rite: Pay a debt of honor to someone who earned better from you, at full value plus a fifth, into their hand, or by transfer or a parcel if they are out of reach.

Question: Whose money or work have you kept, and what is it worth with a fifth added?

Closing: Every debt I owe has a face, and none of them sits in a booth.
