# Day 278: Of Breast-Beating

The Black Breviary, Book III, Assent. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

The Rule of Benedict, written for monks in the sixth century, puts the highest step of humility in the body. Wherever the monk is, at the Work of God, in the oratory, the garden, the field or on the road, sitting or standing, his head is to be bowed and his eyes fixed on the ground, and the Rule gives him the tax collector's words as Benedict remembers them, that he is a sinner not worthy to lift his eyes to heaven. In Luke the man stood far off in the temple with his eyes down, beat his breast and asked for mercy.

Jesus said that man went home justified, and that the Pharisee, who stood apart and thanked his god for his fasts and his tithes, did not. The parable was aimed at the Pharisee's contempt for the man beside him. The churches kept the tax collector's posture and made it a drill. Catholics at Mass strike their breasts at the confession of fault, and the Orthodox repeat his plea on a knotted rope. Benedict adds that the monk should feel the guilt of his sins at every moment and think of himself as already standing at the Judgment, so the bow carries a verdict with it.

Benedict wanted the humility to show to everyone who saw the monk, and anyone who sees a straightened back reads it just as fast. The Pharisee's fault, in Luke's own framing, was trusting in himself and despising others, and he needed the tax collector beside him to measure himself by. A straight back needs nobody down there, and the room you walk into reads it before you speak.

Charge: Walk into every room with your head up.

Rite: Dress in the garment you stand tallest in and walk into the grandest room open to anyone today, a great library, a museum hall, a station concourse. Stand in its middle, shoulders back, and walk one slow circuit.

Question: In front of whom did your body learn to make itself small?

Closing: I meet every eye at its own level and leave the ground to the tax collector.
