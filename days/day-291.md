# Day 291: Of Burdens

The Black Breviary, Book III, Assent. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

A man named Simon was conscripted on the road to the crucifixion, and the verb Matthew uses for it is a soldier's word. It came from the mounted couriers of the Persian king, and Rome adapted the courier system, laying on private persons the duty to supply transport. Simon the Cyrenian was coming in from the country, Mark says, the father of Alexander and Rufus, and the soldiers seized him and made him carry the cross behind Jesus. They took him by the right a courier used to take a farmer's mule. No gospel records a word from him.

The same verb turns up earlier in Matthew, in the sermon that tells anyone compelled to go one mile to go two. The rule was written for men like Simon, and piety has kept it, turning him into the stranger privileged to share the load and giving him a station of his own, the fifth, on the Way of the Cross.

Families and offices still draft whoever happens to be standing nearest, the child made the reliable one at fourteen or the one who manages a parent's moods, the employee whose duty was never in the contract and became theirs by repetition. Carry it well and the sermon's reward is the second mile, more of the same load on the same back.

A burden laid on by compulsion creates no obligation, and whoever relied on yours can find another passer-by or carry it themselves, as John's gospel, which drops Simon altogether, has Jesus go out bearing his own cross.

Charge: Ask who drafted you into each duty, and when.

Rite: Hand back one duty that was laid on you unasked, telling whoever relies on it {From Friday, this is yours}. Then spend the freed hour on your own errand, the application, the pitch or the first page of the work you want your name on.

Question: Which load did you pick up only because you happened to be passing?

Closing: The sermon told the conscript to walk the second mile, and I hand the soldier his beam at the end of the first.
