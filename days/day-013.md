# Day 13: Of the Table

The Black Breviary, Book I, Burden. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

Three men stood near Abraham's tent at Mamre while he sat in the door at the hottest hour, and he ran to meet them and bowed to the ground. He had water brought for their feet, told Sarah to knead cakes from three measures of fine flour, ran to the herd himself for a calf tender and good, and set it before them with curds and milk. Then he stood by them under the tree, and they ate.

Genesis has already said who was eating. The chapter opens with the Lord appearing at Mamre, and before it ends one of the three is speaking as the Lord, and Abraham stands before him to bargain over Sodom. So the one dinner the god of Genesis is ever served is veal set down with curds and milk, at the table of the man his people call their father.

The keepers could not leave it there. The Talmud decided the visitors only appeared to eat, and the Quran tells it with a roasted calf the guests never reach for, so that Abraham grows afraid of them. The law forbade seething a kid in its mother's milk, three times over, and the rabbis built on that verse the separate dishes and the hours of waiting between a steak and a cup of coffee with cream. The patriarch's menu became a crime in his descendants' kitchens.

Genesis closes the verse on a single Hebrew word, they ate, and puts the god's appetite on record a whole book before the law about the kid and its mother. Whoever sets meat beside cream for a guest worth feeding keeps the god's one table in Genesis, and the rule that came after has the god's own supper to argue with.

Charge: Bring an appetite to the table, as the god did.

Rite: Lay the best meat you can afford beside a good cheese or a bowl of cream, and share it with one person you choose. Give them the first piece, then eat your fill.

Question: Which food did you learn to refuse that your teachers' own scripture serves without shame?

Closing: What the god joined at Mamre, meat and milk, I let no rabbi put asunder.
