# Day 364: Of the Feast in the Siege

The Black Breviary, Book III, Assent. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

Isaiah watched Jerusalem hold a feast in a time of siege. The god had called for weeping and mourning, for shaven heads and sackcloth, and instead the city was slaughtering oxen and killing sheep, eating meat and drinking wine, and saying let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die. The prophet hears the god's answer in his own ears, that this sin will not be wiped out of them until they die.

The feasters had named the penalty before the god did. Tomorrow we die was their reason for the meat, and the sentence handed back to them is that same tomorrow, the death the men at the spit had counted on before the prophet opened his mouth.

Paul quoted the feasters to the Corinthians as the conclusion that follows if the dead are not raised, and meant it to sound absurd. Take away the resurrection, as he asks his readers to suppose, and their line stops being a sin and becomes the simplest reading of a short life, and a god whose last threat is the grave has nothing left to hold over anyone who has already accepted it.

Whoever has made that peace is free to kill the ox, and owes the prophets no tears in the meantime. In the same chapter Jerusalem was counting its houses and tearing them down to shore up the defenses and cutting a reservoir for water, and of all the city's labor that day the feast alone was done for the pleasure of the people behind the wall.

Charge: Dress for the feast while the siege is on.

Rite: Answer the call to shaven heads and sackcloth with its opposite. Have your hair cut, washed and dressed today by the best hand you can afford, and leave smelling of whatever they put on it. Say {Tomorrow we die} to the mirror.

Question: Which call to mourning have you obeyed lately, and what pleasure did it cost you?

Closing: Let the prophets keep their sackcloth, and I will keep the table set.
