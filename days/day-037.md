# Day 37: Of Thrones

The Black Breviary, Book I, Burden. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

The lampstand of the tabernacle was hammered from a talent of pure gold, Solomon overlaid his temple with gold down to the floor, and his ivory throne stood on six steps with twelve lions along them. In the book that splendor is the god's own, or lent to the king who sat on the throne of the god.

The plate above gives the fallen a throne of their own, a hall of columns and a long stair with a seat at its summit lit like an altar. Isaiah gave that ambition to the king of Babylon, who said in his heart that he would set his throne above the stars of God, and was promised the pit. Ezekiel dressed the king of Tyre in every precious stone and had the god cast him to the ground because his heart was lifted up by his own beauty. When the daughters of Zion wanted splendor of their own, Isaiah went through their ornaments one by one, the anklets and the crescents, the perfume boxes and the rings, and promised them a stink where the perfume had been.

Each prophet brings the same charge. The offense is splendor worn on the wearer's own account, and the ornament is never the crime. Gold on the lampstand was worship and a bangle on a woman's ankle was pride. The letter to Timothy that sends women out without gold or pearls has been read for centuries beneath a bishop's carved chair, the cathedra that gives every cathedral its name, by clergy in vestments stiff with gold thread. The guilt you were taught to feel at a fine thing guards somebody else's chair.

Charge: Wear your gold without asking anyone for leave.

Rite: Make a throne of the chair you sit in most. Drape it in the richest cloth you own or can buy today, set something gold or brass within reach, and sit in it dressed as if for an audience, receiving no one.

Question: Where in your home have you accepted ugliness as though you deserved it?

Closing: The church gilds its altars and calls my rings vanity, and I keep the rings.
