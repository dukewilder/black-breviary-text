# Day 245: Of the Stable

The Black Breviary, Book III, Assent. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

The ox and the ass at the manger appear nowhere in the gospels. Luke supplies a feeding trough and a lodging with no room, and the animals were brought in later from a line of Isaiah, in which the ox knows its owner and the ass its master's crib, and fixed in place by an apocryphal gospel that has them adore the child. The story that grew up around the manger put the god who took flesh where flesh is fed, among creatures that live for their fodder and the warmth of one another's bodies.

A second-century teacher, later condemned as a heretic, wrote that Jesus ate and drank but never passed what he had eaten, so complete was his self-command. The council held at Chalcedon in the year four hundred fifty-one settled that he was like other men in all things except sin. A thousand years of fast days taught the faithful to treat the stomach as sin's front door, though by the council's own measure its ordinary business lies outside sin. Luke's risen Jesus, among friends who take him for a ghost, shows them his hands and feet and then eats a piece of broiled fish in front of them.

Luke gives the shepherds one sign to know him by, a baby wrapped in cloth and lying in a manger, in a town whose name means house of bread. The sign is the trough. Your body wants what the ox wants, and the hours you give to its wants without apology are the hours in which you are most alive, an animal at ease in its stable.

Charge: Feed the animal first, and feed it well.

Rite: Eat by hand, sitting on the floor, the food you have been calling too rich or too much, bought or cooked for nothing but wanting it. Then lie back where you ate for twenty minutes and answer no errand.

Question: What does your body ask for most often that you still answer with suspicion?

Closing: The ox at the manger never kept a fast day, and I keep its calendar.
