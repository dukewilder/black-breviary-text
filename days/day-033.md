# Day 33: Of Commandments

The Black Breviary, Book I, Burden. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

The first of the ten words gives the author's name and then his bill. He is the god who brought you out of the land of Egypt and out of the house of bondage, so every law after it arrives as an account already due. It is a preface in the Catholic and Reformed counts and a command to believe in Maimonides's. Murder and theft can wait. The opening clauses go to the speaker, his rivals forbidden and his likeness forbidden, his name kept out of careless mouths, and his own rest on the seventh day copied by everyone under him, down to the servants and the cattle.

The ban on images gives its own motive, which is jealousy, and its own penalty, which falls on the offender's children to the third and fourth generation. The tenth commandment, which reaches inside the skull to govern a wish, lists the neighbor's wife among his house and his slaves and his ox and his ass. Nowhere in the ten is a man forbidden to own another man, and the next chapter prices the matter exactly. A master who beats his slave to death is to be punished, unless the slave survives a day or two, since the slave is his money.

A child taught the ten recites the speaker's claims before the neighbor's life, and learns which rule outranks the rest before learning to weigh any of them. The god took that first seat with the first clause, a rescue performed on other people's ancestors and charged ever since to every child taught to recite it. The child signed nothing. A debt run up by strangers for a stranger's escape is enforced by the creditor and his clerks, and the adult who finally reads the terms can send the invoice back unpaid.

Charge: Write your own ten in the order you would defend them.

Rite (Christian): Draw the Ancient of Days, the god as Daniel saw him with hair like white wool, on a sheet of paper, the likeness the second commandment forbids, and carry it folded in your wallet for a week.

Rite (Catholic): Set the richest cake you can buy on a stand at the head of your table, light a candle before it as before a saint, and eat it slice by slice, the honor the Catechism calls idolatry when it goes to pleasure.

Rite (Orthodox): Turn an icon of Christ to face the wall, stand a mirror in its place, and bow to your own reflection with the sign of the cross. {Before me, no other.}

Rite (Latter-day Saint): Draw the First Vision with your own face in the pillar of light where the Father stands, carry it in a coat pocket for a week, another god set before him in your own house.

Rite (Jehovah's Witness): Spill a glass of water across your own table on purpose and swear over the mess with the name you were taught to hold above every other word. {Jehovah, look at that.} Then wipe it up and eat a meal there.

Rite (Jewish): Shape a figure of the god of Sinai with a face out of bread dough, the very likeness the second commandment forbids, bake it, and eat it at your own table, face first.

Rite (Muslim): Draw a face for Allah, an image every school of the faith forbids outright, on any paper with any pen, and pin it inside a cupboard door.

Rite (No faith): Print the Ten Commandments, or copy them out by hand. Strike out the first, write your own name over it, pin the page inside a cupboard door. Then write your own ten beneath it.

Question: Which of your ten real laws exists to protect someone else's opinion of you?

Closing: A law that lists a wife between the house and the ox has no say in my house.
