# Day 326: Of Looking Away

The Black Breviary, Book III, Assent. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

The last verse of Isaiah sends the worshipers out of Jerusalem to look at the corpses of the men who rebelled, whose worm does not die and whose fire is not quenched, and to find them loathsome. On the Sabbath of the new month the synagogue reads the chapter aloud and repeats the verse before at the close, so that the reading ends on worship, and the corpses are still read aloud one verse earlier.

Crowds gathered for the Inquisition's burnings in Seville and the burnings at Smithfield, and food vendors worked the crowds at the Tyburn hangings, and for most of history a public death was a show a pious audience could attend with a clear conscience, since the suffering had been certified as deserved.

A person can now spend hours a day watching suffering they can do nothing about, a war on another continent, a flood in a town they will never visit, and call it conscience or staying informed. The feeling is the one Isaiah handed his worshipers on the walk out to the corpses, a place among the righteous at the edge of someone else's ruin, and the platform sells it by the minute with an advertisement beside each death. An evening given to the flood teaches the shape of every collapsed roof and the name of one rescued dog. The dog was saved without you. Pity follows the eyes, and eyes that spent the evening on a drowned town an ocean away left the sick friend down the street alone with a cold supper.

Charge: Refuse your eyes to suffering you cannot touch.

Rite: Delete or log out of the app you watch the world's disasters on, and take the hour it would have had to someone near you in trouble, carrying a meal, cash or a pair of working hands.

Question: What have you won by watching the suffering you watch most?

Closing: A stranger's flood on a screen has no claim on my evening.
