# Day 339: Of the Pelican

The Black Breviary, Book III, Assent. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

Medieval bestiaries told of a pelican whose chicks had died, and who tore open her own breast and brought them back with her blood. The Church took the bird for an emblem of Christ and the Eucharist and carved her on altars and on the doors of tabernacles, and a eucharistic hymn still sung before the reserved sacrament calls Christ the loving pelican and begs to be washed clean in his blood. The zoology was false, and the picture of the parent who gives her own body to the young stayed on the altar anyway.

Paul had called himself the Corinthians' father in the faith, one father among ten thousand instructors, and in his second letter he told them that parents should lay up for their children, that he sought them rather than their goods, and that he for his part would most gladly spend and be spent for them, though the more he loved them the less he was loved, which is the pelican's gift in a father's mouth.

A parent takes the first portion when the dinner is cut. The coat bought for no one else, with the wool warm against the collarbone, the bath run hot enough to redden the shins, a room with a lock and a plate carried into it, are the upkeep of the one adult the children can see. Children who watch a parent eat the good end of the loaf see a grown life enjoyed, with the butter still soft on the knife, a picture the pelican never gave them.

Charge: Spend on yourself what you were taught to spend for others.

Rite: Spend money today on a pleasure nobody else will share, and take it alone for two hours. Then claim a standing outing of your own, a regular hour out of the house, and announce it to anyone you live with as already begun.

Question: Which piece of the life you have handed over entirely to others would you take back first?

Closing: Whoever I love gets me in one piece, and the pelican can keep her wound.
