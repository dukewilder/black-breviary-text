# Day 193: Of Search

The Black Breviary, Book II, Refusal. All 365 days: https://blackbreviary.app/llms/days.md

A verse of the Quran tells believers not to ask about things which, if they were made plain to them, would distress them. The next verse recalls a people before them who asked such questions and then lost their faith over the answers. It does not call the answers false.

Rome built a ban on questions into an office. Its Index of forbidden books held the treatise that set the earth moving around the sun from sixteen sixteen, suspended until corrected, and struck it from the list only in the edition of eighteen thirty-five, long after the astronomers had stopped arguing. Catholics who obeyed spent two centuries reading the founding book of their own sky, if they read it at all, in copies corrected by hand to call the moving earth a hypothesis.

The Quran warns the asker of distress and sets no penalty, so its caution guards the asker, and in one traditional account the question that prompted it was whether the pilgrimage was owed every year. Rome's Index guarded the keeper. It fenced off the knowledge that would weaken him, and the penalty he set for asking measures the loss he feared, an earth the psalm had promised could not be moved.

Some congregations and households keep a forbidden question, about a vote taken in a closed meeting or a letter nobody answered, and the penalty set on asking measures what an honest answer would cost the keepers. A court archive or a library will often hold the record, and the asker walks out with a photocopy no elder can take back.

Charge: Ask who a ban on a question protects.

Rite: Write down the question you were raised never to ask, find its answer today in a source the faith did not publish, and copy what you find under it in ink. Sign the page and say {Asked and answered.}

Question: What were you protected from knowing, and what did the protection cost you?

Closing: I ask who a ban protects, and the earth goes on moving under my chair.
